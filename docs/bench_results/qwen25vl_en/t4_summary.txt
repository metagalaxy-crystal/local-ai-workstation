**Summary:**
The Silk Road, named by 19th-century German geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen, was a network of trade and cultural-exchange routes connecting China with Central Asia, Western Asia, and the Mediterranean. It was particularly significant during the Tang dynasty, when tens of thousands of foreign merchants and students lived in Chang'an, enriching Chinese culture with foreign influences. The maritime Silk Road, centered around ports like Guangzhou and Quanzhou, was equally prosperous and facilitated trade with Southeast Asia and East Africa. Despite its decline after the Ming dynasty, the Silk Road left behind significant cultural and historical artifacts, and in 2014, a section of it was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List.

**Key Points:**
1. **Cultural Exchange:** The Silk Road facilitated the spread of technology, religions, and artistic forms between China and the Western world.
2. **Economic Prosperity:** Both the overland and maritime Silk Roads were key in trade and cultural exchange, especially during the Tang dynasty.
3. **Heritage and Preservation:** The cultural and historical remnants left by the Silk Road, such as Dunhuang murals, Mogao Caves, and the inscription of a section on UNESCO's World Heritage List, continue to be significant.
