Summary (78 words):
The Silk Road was a network of trade routes linking China to Central, Western Asia, and the Mediterranean, named after the silk trade by 19th-century geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen. It facilitated cultural exchange, spreading technologies like papermaking and Buddhism eastward, and goods like spices and grapes westward. The overland route peaked during the Tang dynasty, while the maritime Silk Road thrived from the Tang to Song dynasties, featuring ports like Guangzhou and Quanzhou. Decline followed the Ming dynasty due to maritime bans and political shifts, but its legacy endures in artifacts and UNESCO recognition.

Key Points:
- The Silk Road facilitated extensive cultural and technological exchange between East and West, including goods, religions, and innovations.
- The overland route reached its peak during the Tang dynasty, while the maritime Silk Road was prominent from the Tang to Song dynasties.
- Despite its decline after the Ming dynasty, the Silk Road's legacy is preserved in cultural artifacts and recognized by UNESCO.
