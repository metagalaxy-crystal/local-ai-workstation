**Summary (98 words):**
The Silk Road was an ancient network of trade and cultural-exchange routes connecting China with Central Asia, Western Asia, and the Mediterranean. Named by Ferdinand von Richthofen in the 19th century due to silk trade, it initially served as an overland route. Its significance extended beyond goods, facilitating the westward spread of innovations like papermaking and gunpowder, and the eastward flow of crops, religions, and arts. The maritime Silk Road emerged later, exporting porcelain and tea. While the overland route declined post-Ming due to maritime bans and Ottoman interference, its legacy endures in cultural artifacts and was recognized by UNESCO in 2014.

**3 Key Points:**

1. **Beyond Trade:** The Silk Road facilitated not just commerce but profound cultural and technological exchanges, spreading innovations (paper, gunpowder) westward and cultural/agricultural goods eastward.
2. **Dual Routes:** It operated as both an overland network and a maritime route; the latter shifted exports from silk to porcelain and tea, earning the "Porcelain Road" nickname.
3. **Legacy & Recognition:** Despite declining after the Ming dynasty due to political shifts and Ottoman interference, the Silk Road's Eurasian exchange legacy was preserved and officially recognized by UNESCO in 2014.
